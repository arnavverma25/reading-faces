The future and conclusions

Where is this heading, and what would I build today with what the research already offers?

2 minute readConclusions
Read the narration text

Chapter nine, the future and conclusions.

The first open question is the cue itself. A spoken word is slow and it talks over the conversation. Research with blind and low-vision participants suggests a mix: a vibration for something that lasts, and a short sound for something that happens once. And only a few different cues.

The research is moving in four directions. Using Action Units to make the result easier to explain. Learning from unlabelled video. Detecting covers the system has never seen. And saving energy by looking at fewer frames. What is still missing is a test with blind and low-vision users in real conversations.

What would I build? A camera on glasses, with the computing on the phone. A small network for the label. A view check that makes the system stay quiet when the face is covered or turned. Smoothing over about half a second. And a short sound that means smiling, not happy.

Thank you for following this tutorial. On the next page you can try the ideas yourself in the simulator and take the quiz.

Better cues than a spoken word

A spoken word is slow and covers what the partner is saying. The study from Chapter 1 used a different mix [1]: a vibration for a state that lasts (eye contact) and a short sound for something that happens once (a nod). Participants liked that a vibration did not interrupt their listening, and found sounds better for telling cues apart.

CueTime it takesHow many meaningsDisturbs listeningBest for
Spoken wordLongMany, no learning neededYesFirst use, and rare events
Short soundShortA few, must be learnedA littleEvents: a smile starts, surprise
VibrationShortVery fewNoLasting states: “face in view”, “still smiling”

This table is my own summary. The clear research finding is the limit on quantity: five cues were too many [1].

Where the research is going

  • Action Units as a helper. The 2024 survey names AU-assisted recognition as a direction because it is easier to explain [2], and open toolkits now deliver AUs in real time [8].
  • Learning from unlabelled video raised video accuracy by 5 to 6 points over earlier supervised methods [12]. Next, such models must shrink.
  • Covers never seen before. Detectors trained only on clean faces can flag any object in front of the face [17].
  • Less energy per decision. Adaptive frame rates [14] suit wearables. I found no assistive device that uses them yet.
  • Tests with real users. The assistive prototypes stop at classifier accuracy [5], [6]. Studies with BLV users in everyday conversation are the missing piece.

Groups to follow

Shiri Azenkot's group at Cornell Tech (accessible non-verbal cues [1]), Andrey Savchenko at HSE University (expression recognition on phones [9]), and Mohammad Soleymani's group at the University of Southern California (the open LibreFace toolkit [8]).

What I would build today

  1. Camera on glasses, computing on the phone. Best view, enough battery, no video leaves the user's devices.
  2. A small trained network for the label, about the size of EfficientNet-B0, quantized.
  3. A view check first: face size, head angle and a small cover detector. When it fails, the device says so.
  4. Smoothing over about half a second, with fewer frames when the face is steady.
  5. Two or three cues that describe: a sound for “smiling”, one for “surprised”, a vibration for “I cannot see the face”.

Five things to remember

1. The photo case is solved; the conversation case is not.

Above 92% on clean test photos, near 67% on natural ones.

2. Time is part of the method.

Smoothing makes a flickering label usable, at the price of a short delay.

3. Knowing when not to answer is a feature.

Covered and turned faces should lower the trust or silence the device.

4. The network is not the slow part.

Waiting to be sure and speaking take longer.

5. A face movement is not a feeling.

The honest output is “smiling”, and the user decides what it means.
