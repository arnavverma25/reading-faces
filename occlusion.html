Covered faces and side views

Glasses, masks, hair, beards, hands and turned heads all hide part of the face. A useful system has to notice this, cope where it can, and stay quiet where it cannot.

3 minute readOcclusionHead pose
Read the narration text

Chapter five, covered faces and side views.

Each cover hides different Action Units. Sunglasses hide the eyes and brows, so surprise and fear are hard to see. A mask hides the mouth, so the smile is lost. Long hair can hide the brows or one side of the face, and a beard hides the lip corners.

This hurts people as well as machines. In a psychology study, surgical masks lowered human recognition by thirty-one percent. Anger was not affected, because the brows stay visible.

One important detail: a landmark model still places every point when the mouth is covered. It simply guesses. So you need a separate check to notice that something is in the way.

There are three ways to cope. Region attention cuts the face into regions and learns how much to trust each one. Detect, mask and reconstruct rebuilds the hidden part before classifying. And the simplest way uses only the visible half of the face. For a wearable I prefer region attention, and I always tell the user when the face is partly covered.

A turned head is a cover too, because the far side of the face disappears. My rule: up to thirty degrees, classify normally. Between thirty and sixty, trust the result less. Beyond sixty degrees, stay quiet.

How much does a cover hurt?

It hurts people. With 220 participants, real surgical masks lowered expression recognition by 31%. Anger was not affected, because lowered brows stay visible. Disgust and sadness suffered most [15].

It hurts machines. The authors of the Region Attention Network (RAN) marked by hand every face in three test sets that was covered at the top, bottom or side, or by glasses or a mask. On those faces a plain network scored 80.19% on RAF-DB, 73.33% on FERPlus and 49.48% on AffectNet [16].

A trap: landmarks do not tell you A landmark model places all its points even when the mouth is behind a mask. It guesses. The Face Landmarker's result has landmarks, blendshapes and a head matrix, but nothing that says “this point is hidden” [4]. Noticing a cover needs its own step.

First notice the cover, then cope with it

To notice a cover you can add a tiny classifier for “clear, mask or sunglasses”, use a detector that has learned only what clean face patches look like and flags everything else [17], or simply treat a low smoothed score as “unclear”.

My choice, and when I would choose differently Method A for a wearable: one network, modest extra cost, any kind of cover. Method C where masks are the rule, as in a hospital. I would avoid B, because it shows the classifier a mouth the camera never saw. In every case the cue should add “partly covered”.

Side views

A turned head is a cover too: the far side goes out of view.

The RAN paper also tested faces turned or tilted by more than 30° and 45°. A plain network scored 84.04% and 83.15% on those RAF-DB faces, and 50.10% and 48.50% on AffectNet; region attention added 2 to 5 points [16]. The sets are small (558 RAF-DB faces beyond 45°) and none covers a full side view, so behaviour near 90° is not well known.

Measuring the angle costs nothing extra. The Face Landmarker returns a transformation matrix per face [4], and its rotation part gives the turn to the side (yaw) and the tilt (pitch). Chapter 6 shows the code.

  • Up to 30°: classify as normal.
  • 30° to 60°: trust each frame less and smooth for longer.
  • Over 60°: stay quiet and tell the user “face turned away”.

The 30° boundary follows [16]; the 60° one is my own suggestion.
