From frame to label

Getting from a camera frame to one word takes six steps. This chapter walks through them, introduces Action Units, and compares the main ways to do the classification step.

3 minute readAlgorithms
Read the narration text

Chapter three, from frame to label.

The software works in six steps. It finds the face. It places landmarks on the eyes, brows, nose and mouth. It checks whether the view is good enough. It classifies the expression. It smooths the result over time. And it gives the cue to the user.

To describe expressions, researchers use the Facial Action Coding System. It breaks every expression into numbered muscle movements called Action Units. For example, Action Unit twelve pulls the lip corners up, which is a smile. Some units are on the upper face and some on the lower face. That matters later, because a mask hides the lower ones and sunglasses hide the upper ones.

There are three ways to classify. You can detect Action Units and apply rules. You can feed the face picture to a small neural network. Or you can use a large transformer. For a phone, I recommend the small network. It is almost as accurate as the large model, and it runs in a fraction of a second.

One warning about accuracy. Even human annotators agree on only about sixty percent of the images in the main test set.

The six steps

  1. Find the face. A small detector returns a box around each face.
  2. Place landmarks. Google's Face Landmarker returns 478 three-dimensional points, 52 “blendshape” scores (numbers from 0 to 1, such as how far the jaw is open) and a matrix describing how the head is turned [4].
  3. Check the view. Is the face big enough, facing the camera and uncovered? If not, stop here (Chapter 5).
  4. Classify the expression. One score per label. The rest of this chapter is about this step.
  5. Smooth over time. Many frames become one steady label (Chapter 4).
  6. Give the cue, but only when something has changed.

Action Units: an alphabet for the face

The Facial Action Coding System (FACS) describes any expression as a set of numbered muscle movements called Action Units (AUs). AU12 pulls the lip corners up. AU4 lowers the brows.

The CK+ dataset used these rules to decide which label a coded expression could receive [7].

LabelAction Units that must be presentPart of the face
HappyAU12Lower
SadnessAU1 + AU4 + AU15Upper and lower
SurpriseAU1 + AU2, or AU5Upper
FearAU1 + AU2 + AU4Upper
AngerAU23 and AU24 (tight, pressed lips)Lower
DisgustAU9 or AU10Middle
ContemptAU14Lower
Why this table matters later Cover the mouth and the rule for “happy” has nothing to work with. Cover the eyes and brows and “surprise” and “fear” are gone.

Three ways to classify

RouteHow it worksReported accuracySpeed and size
A. Action Units, then rulesMeasure AUs or blendshapes, then apply rules like the table above.LibreFace detects AUs with an F1 score of 62.0 on BP4D [8]. AU errors pass on to the label.About 27 frames per second on a CPU [8]
B. Small networkGive the face crop to a compact network such as MobileNet or EfficientNet.AffectNet, 7 labels: 64.7% (MobileNet) to 66.3% (EfficientNet-B2) [9]16 ms and 14 MB up to 191 ms and 30 MB per face on an Android phone [9]
C. Large transformerA vision transformer looks at the face, sometimes with the landmarks.POSTER: 92.05% on RAF-DB, 67.31% on AffectNet [2]A recent example: 86 million parameters, 331 MB [6]
  • Route A is the easiest to explain and can ignore hidden AUs, but it stacks two sources of error.
  • Route B fits a phone best: small, fast, trained end to end. You cannot see why it chose a label.
  • Route C is the most accurate, by about one point on AffectNet (67.3% against 66.3%), at roughly ten times the file size.
My choice, and when I would choose differently Route B for the label, plus the landmarks from step 2 for the view check. Route A when the face is half covered and only some AUs can be trusted. Route C only when a server is acceptable.

How good is “good”?

People do not agree either: two annotators labelling the same AffectNet images agreed on 60.7% of them [10]. A model at 66% is near the point where the labels themselves are uncertain. Yet the best models pass 92% on RAF-DB [2]. The score depends on the test set as much as on the model (Chapter 7).

The test sets named in this tutorial AffectNet, RAF-DB and FERPlus are collections of natural face photos with expression labels. AffectNet is the largest and has the lowest scores. DFEW is a collection of short movie clips.
