Chapter 1 of 9

The non-verbal gap

Sighted people read faces all the time without noticing it. This chapter describes what is missing for a person who cannot see the face, and what exactly a phone would have to do about it.

2 minute readProblem definition
Read the narration text

Chapter one, the non-verbal gap.

When people talk face to face, the words are only part of the message. A smile, a frown or raised eyebrows tell you how your words were received. A person who is blind or has low vision does not get these signals.

Research shows that giving such cues back helps. In a 2024 study, blind and low-vision participants judged much better whether a partner was paying attention when they received extra cues. The same study also found that too many cues at once are hard to follow.

So the task is this. A camera films the conversation partner. Software on the phone decides which expression is on the face. The user gets one short cue, within about a second.

The easy version of this problem uses one clear photo from the front. A real conversation is video, with covered faces, turned heads and poor light. Those hard cases are what the rest of this tutorial is about.

Finally, keep two things apart. An expression is what the face does. An emotion is what the person feels. The camera only sees the first one.

What gets lost

In a conversation, words carry only part of the message. A smile shows that a joke landed; a frown shows doubt. A person who is blind or has low vision (BLV from here on) has to guess from the voice alone.

Giving the signals back helps. In a 2024 study, 16 BLV participants held conversations in virtual reality with and without extra cues for eye contact, nodding and head shaking. With the cues, they judged correctly whether a partner was paying attention 87.5% of the time. Without them the figure was 68.8% [1]. The same study gives a warning: its first design had five cues, including smiling and frowning, and that was too many to follow while talking [1].

Two lessons for the design The cue is worth giving, and it must be small: few signals, short, and easy to ignore.

The task, stated exactly

InputLive video from a camera the user wears or holds, pointed at the conversation partner.
OutputOne of a few labels (smiling, sad, angry, surprised, neutral) or “not sure”.
TimingAbout one second from camera to cue. This is my design target.
Where it runsOn the phone. The video shows other people and should not go to a server.
HonestyThe system must notice when it cannot see the face, because the user cannot check.

Why one clear photo is the easy version

Most examples online label one sharp, front-facing photo. That works well, and it is not our problem. A real conversation differs in four ways.

  • It is video. Expressions come and go within seconds (Chapter 4).
  • The face is often partly covered by glasses, a mask, hair, a beard or a hand (Chapter 5).
  • People turn their heads, so a worn camera rarely gets a front view (Chapter 5).
  • The picture is poor: bad light, a small face, a moving camera (Chapter 2).

A 2024 survey of the field lists the same things (occlusion, head pose, lighting, hairstyle, accessories) as the disturbances a robust system must survive [2].

Three terms to keep apart

Expression and emotion

An expression is what the face does. An emotion is what the person feels. A camera sees only the first (Chapter 8).

Ordinary and micro

A smile lasts about 0.5 to 4 seconds. A micro-expression is involuntary and lasts 0.065 to 0.5 seconds [3]. The two are often mixed up.

FER

Facial expression recognition, the name of this research field.
