This page is the annotated bibliography.

The references are listed in the order in which they first appear in the tutorial, and that is where the numbers come from. Wherever you see a number in square brackets in the text, it is a link to the matching entry on this page.

Each entry gives the title, the authors, where and when it was published, and a link. Below that is a synopsis in my own words, and a reliability note that explains how far I trust the source and why.

Most of the sources are peer-reviewed papers from 2020 to 2026. A few are older, because they introduced a dataset or a set of rules that every later paper still uses. Two are preprints, which I have marked as such. And one is the text of a law.

How to read an entry Title, authors, source and date come first, then a link. Synopsis says what the source contains. Reliability is my own rating (high, medium-high or medium) with the reason. The last line lists the chapters that cite it.
  1. [1] “Accessible Nonverbal Cues to Support Conversations in VR for Blind and Low Vision People”, C. Jung, J. I. Segal, J. Collins, A. S. Won, R. E. Gonzalez Penuela, and S. Azenkot. Proceedings of the 26th International ACM SIGACCESS Conference on Computers and Accessibility (ASSETS '24). October 2024.

    https://doi.org/10.1145/3663548.3675663

    Synopsis: A user study with 16 blind and low-vision people who held two 15-minute conversations in virtual reality, one with extra cues and one without. Eye contact was given as a vibration, and head nods and head shakes as short sounds. With the cues, participants judged more often correctly whether a partner was paying attention to them (87.5% against 68.8%). The first design had five cues, including smiling and frowning, and participants found that too much to follow.

    Reliability: High. ASSETS is the main peer-reviewed research conference on accessibility, and the authors are an established accessibility research group at Cornell. The study is in virtual reality, so it shows that the cues help and how they should be delivered, not how well a camera can detect them in real life.

    Cited in: The non-verbal gap, The future and conclusions

  2. [2] “A Survey on Facial Expression Recognition of Static and Dynamic Emotions”, Y. Wang, S. Yan, Y. Liu, W. Song, J. Liu, Y. Chang, X. Mai, X. Hu, W. Zhang, and Z. Gan. arXiv preprint arXiv:2408.15777. August 2024.

    https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15777

    Synopsis: A large review of deep-learning facial expression recognition. It covers image-based methods and video-based methods separately, groups them by the problem they solve (occlusion and head pose, noisy labels, frame sampling, and others), and collects benchmark tables for the common datasets. It also lists open problems and future directions.

    Reliability: Medium-high. It is a preprint, so it had not passed journal peer review in the version I read. I use it mainly for its benchmark tables and its list of open problems, which point back to peer-reviewed papers, and I checked several of the numbers against the original papers.

    Cited in: The non-verbal gap, From frame to label, Video and changing emotions, Successes and failures, The future and conclusions

  3. [3] “Video-Based Facial Micro-Expression Analysis: A Survey of Datasets, Features and Algorithms”, X. Ben, Y. Ren, J. Zhang, S.-J. Wang, K. Kpalma, W. Meng, and Y.-J. Liu. IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, vol. 44, no. 9. 2022 (online March 2021).

    https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2021.3067464

    Synopsis: A review of micro-expression research. It defines micro-expressions and how they differ from ordinary expressions in duration and strength, describes the datasets and the high-speed cameras used to record them, and explains why they are hard to detect.

    Reliability: High. A survey in one of the top peer-reviewed journals in computer vision.

    Cited in: The non-verbal gap, Sensor possibilities, Video and changing emotions

  4. [4] “Face landmark detection guide (MediaPipe Face Landmarker) and Post-training quantization (LiteRT)”, Google. Google AI Edge developer documentation. No publication date; read on 5 October 2026.

    https://ai.google.dev/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/face_landmarker
    https://developers.google.com/edge/litert/conversion/tensorflow/quantization/post_training_quantization

    Synopsis: Official documentation for an on-device face pipeline. The Face Landmarker page lists what the task returns (478 three-dimensional landmarks, 52 blendshape scores and a face transformation matrix), the three models inside it with their input sizes, and the Python interface used in my code. The quantization page explains how to shrink a trained model and gives the expected size and speed gains.

    Reliability: High for facts about the software, because it is written by the team that maintains it. It is documentation, not research: it gives no accuracy numbers for expressions and it changes without notice.

    Cited in: Sensor possibilities, From frame to label, Covered faces and side views, Algorithm and code, The challenges

  5. [5] “Masked Face Emotion Recognition Based on Facial Landmarks and Deep Learning Approaches for Visually Impaired People”, M. Mukhiddinov, O. Djuraev, F. Akhmedov, A. Mukhamadiyev, and J. Cho. Sensors, vol. 23, no. 3, article 1080. January 2023.

    https://doi.org/10.3390/s23031080

    Synopsis: Describes an emotion recognition method made for smart glasses used by visually impaired people. It first brightens dark images, then covers the lower face with a synthetic mask, and classifies the emotion from the upper face only, using landmark positions, gradient features and a convolutional network. It reports 69.3% accuracy on AffectNet. The glasses and phone only capture video; a server does the processing and returns the result as speech.

    Reliability: Medium-high. A peer-reviewed open-access journal paper with a clear method. The weak points are that the masks are synthetic, processing speed is not reported, and no blind or low-vision users tested the system.

    Cited in: Sensor possibilities, Covered faces and side views, Successes and failures, The challenges, The future and conclusions

  6. [6] “Facial Emotion Recognition Through a Smart Glasses Prototype: Improving Social Interaction for Visually Impaired Users Through Enhanced Deep Learning CBAM Architectures”, N. Yalcin and M. Alisawi. Applied Sciences, vol. 16, no. 9, article 4415. April 2026.

    https://doi.org/10.3390/app16094415

    Synopsis: Trains two attention-based classifiers (a vision transformer and a FaceNet-based network) for a planned smart-glasses assistant. The transformer reaches about 98% on the authors' own cleaned test set but about 72% on AffectNet, and the second model fails on outside real-world images. The paper measures model speed on a GPU and states openly what is still missing.

    Reliability: Medium. Peer-reviewed and very recent, but the reported results are for the classifier only. The authors state that the study does not include visually impaired participants and that end-to-end delay on the glasses was not measured.

    Cited in: Sensor possibilities, From frame to label, Successes and failures, The challenges, The future and conclusions

  7. [7] “The Extended Cohn-Kanade Dataset (CK+): A Complete Dataset for Action Unit and Emotion-Specified Expression”, P. Lucey, J. F. Cohn, T. Kanade, J. Saragih, Z. Ambadar, and I. Matthews. IEEE Computer Society Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition Workshops (CVPRW). June 2010.

    https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5543262

    Synopsis: Presents a dataset of posed expression videos in which trained coders marked the facial Action Units. It includes a table of the Action Unit rules that an expression had to meet to receive each emotion label, which I use in Chapter 3.

    Reliability: High, but old. It is one of the most cited dataset papers in this field and its authors include the researchers who built the original Cohn-Kanade data. I use it only for the Action Unit rules, which have not changed. Its posed, front-view recordings are exactly the easy case this tutorial argues is not enough.

    Cited in: From frame to label

  8. [8] “LibreFace: An Open-Source Toolkit for Deep Facial Expression Analysis”, D. Chang, Y. Yin, Z. Li, M. Tran, and M. Soleymani. IEEE/CVF Winter Conference on Applications of Computer Vision (WACV). January 2024.

    https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2024/html/Chang_LibreFace_An_Open-Source_Toolkit_for_Deep_Facial_Expression_Analysis_WACV_2024_paper.html

    Synopsis: An open-source toolkit that detects Action Units, estimates their intensity and classifies expressions. A large model teaches a small ResNet-18 so that it runs in real time on a CPU. The paper reports accuracy for each task and compares speed with the older OpenFace 2.0 toolkit.

    Reliability: High. Peer-reviewed conference paper from a university research lab (University of Southern California), with public code so the results can be checked.

    Cited in: From frame to label, The future and conclusions

  9. [9] “Classifying Emotions and Engagement in Online Learning Based on a Single Facial Expression Recognition Neural Network”, A. V. Savchenko, L. V. Savchenko, and I. Makarov. IEEE Transactions on Affective Computing, vol. 13, no. 4, pp. 2132-2143. 2022.

    https://doi.org/10.1109/TAFFC.2022.3188390
    https://github.com/av-savchenko/face-emotion-recognition

    Synopsis: Shows that one small network, first trained to recognise identities and then fine-tuned on AffectNet, gives face features that work for emotion and engagement recognition in video. The authors' public code repository lists each released model with its AffectNet accuracy, file size and the time one prediction takes on an Android phone. Those phone measurements are the ones quoted in this tutorial.

    Reliability: High. Published in the leading journal for affective computing. The speed and size numbers come from the authors' own repository, not from the paper text, so they are self-reported and tied to one phone model.

    Cited in: From frame to label, Algorithm and code, Successes and failures, The challenges, The future and conclusions

  10. [10] “AffectNet: A Database for Facial Expression, Valence, and Arousal Computing in the Wild”, A. Mollahosseini, B. Hasani, and M. H. Mahoor. IEEE Transactions on Affective Computing, vol. 10, no. 1. 2019.

    https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03985

    Synopsis: Introduces AffectNet, more than one million face images collected from the web, about 450,000 of them labelled by hand. When two annotators labelled the same images, they agreed on 60.7% of them.

    Reliability: High, but older. It is the dataset paper for the benchmark that most systems in this tutorial are tested on, published in the leading journal of the field. I use it only for the annotator agreement figure.

    Cited in: From frame to label, The challenges

  11. [11] “Frame-level Prediction of Facial Expressions, Valence, Arousal and Action Units for Mobile Devices”, A. V. Savchenko. arXiv preprint arXiv:2203.13436; entry to the 3rd Affective Behavior Analysis in-the-wild (ABAW) competition at the CVPR 2022 workshops. March 2022.

    https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.13436

    Synopsis: Classifies every video frame with a small EfficientNet and then smooths the frame predictions over time. On the competition's validation videos, averaging over 15 frames raised the expression F1 score from 0.381 to 0.402. The paper also reports the AffectNet accuracy of the small backbones.

    Reliability: Medium-high. A competition paper by a well-known author in mobile expression recognition, with public code. Competition papers are short and tuned to one dataset, so the size of the gain may differ elsewhere.

    Cited in: Video and changing emotions, Algorithm and code

  12. [12] “MAE-DFER: Efficient Masked Autoencoder for Self-supervised Dynamic Facial Expression Recognition”, L. Sun, Z. Lian, B. Liu, and J. Tao. Proceedings of the 31st ACM International Conference on Multimedia (MM '23), pp. 6110-6121. October 2023.

    https://doi.org/10.1145/3581783.3612365

    Synopsis: A video transformer that first learns from about one million unlabelled face clips by rebuilding hidden parts of the video, and is then fine-tuned for expression recognition on 16-frame clips. It reports results on three in-the-wild video datasets, per-class accuracy, and its computing cost.

    Reliability: High. Peer-reviewed at a major multimedia conference, with public code and widely used as a comparison point by later papers.

    Cited in: Video and changing emotions, Algorithm and code, Successes and failures, The challenges, The future and conclusions

  13. [13] “DFEW: A Large-Scale Database for Recognizing Dynamic Facial Expressions in the Wild”, X. Jiang, Y. Zong, W. Zheng, C. Tang, W. Xia, C. Lu, and J. Liu. Proceedings of the 28th ACM International Conference on Multimedia (MM '20). October 2020.

    https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.05924

    Synopsis: Introduces a video dataset of 16,372 short clips cut from more than 1,500 movies, each labelled by ten annotators with one of seven expressions. It defines the two scores used by later video papers (WAR and UAR) and gives a first baseline.

    Reliability: High. Peer-reviewed, and it is the standard benchmark for video expression recognition. One caution for this topic: movie clips are acted and filmed by professionals, so they are easier to see than a view from a chest or glasses camera.

    Cited in: Video and changing emotions

  14. [14] “Facial Expression Recognition with Adaptive Frame Rate based on Multiple Testing Correction”, A. Savchenko. Proceedings of the 40th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), PMLR vol. 202, pp. 30119-30129. July 2023.

    https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/savchenko23a.html

    Synopsis: Proposes looking at only a few frames of a video segment first and adding more frames only when the decision is not yet reliable. A statistical test decides when to stop. The method works with any frame-level network without retraining and is reported to be 1.5 to 40 times faster than processing every frame.

    Reliability: High. ICML is one of the top peer-reviewed machine learning conferences. The speed-ups are measured in computation saved, not in battery life on a real phone.

    Cited in: Video and changing emotions, Algorithm and code, The challenges, The future and conclusions

  15. [15] “The Recognition of Facial Expressions Under Surgical Masks: The Primacy of Anger”, A. M. Proverbio and A. Cerri. Frontiers in Neuroscience, vol. 16, article 864490. 2022.

    https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.864490

    Synopsis: A psychology experiment in which 220 students rated photographs of eight actors showing seven emotional states, with and without a real surgical mask. Masks lowered recognition by 31% overall. Anger was not affected, while disgust and sadness suffered most.

    Reliability: High for what it measures. Peer-reviewed, with a clear design and a large group. It tests people, not software, and uses posed photographs.

    Cited in: Covered faces and side views, Successes and failures

  16. [16] “Region Attention Networks for Pose and Occlusion Robust Facial Expression Recognition”, K. Wang, X. Peng, J. Yang, D. Meng, and Y. Qiao. IEEE Transactions on Image Processing, vol. 29, pp. 4057-4069. 2020.

    https://doi.org/10.1109/TIP.2019.2956143

    Synopsis: Builds test sets of occluded faces and turned faces by hand-labelling images from three in-the-wild datasets, then proposes a network that crops several face regions and learns how much to trust each one. It reports accuracy for a plain network and for the region network on every occlusion and pose subset.

    Reliability: High. Peer-reviewed in a leading image processing journal. Its occlusion and pose test sets became a standard benchmark that later papers, including Latent-OFER, still use.

    Cited in: Covered faces and side views, Successes and failures, The challenges

  17. [17] “Latent-OFER: Detect, Mask, and Reconstruct with Latent Vectors for Occluded Facial Expression Recognition”, I. Lee, E. Lee, and S. B. Yoo. IEEE/CVF International Conference on Computer Vision (ICCV). October 2023.

    https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.11404

    Synopsis: Handles a covered face in three steps: find the covered patches, blank them out, and rebuild them before classifying. The detector learns only from clean face patches, so it can flag objects it has never seen. The paper reports results on normal and occluded test sets.

    Reliability: High. ICCV is a top peer-reviewed computer vision conference. The authors themselves note that the rebuilding step does not transfer well to data unlike its training set.

    Cited in: Covered faces and side views, Successes and failures, The challenges, The future and conclusions

  18. [18] “Faces of Fairness: Examining Bias in Facial Expression Recognition Datasets and Models”, M. M. Hosseini, A. P. Fard, and M. H. Mahoor. arXiv preprint arXiv:2502.11049. February 2025.

    https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11049

    Synopsis: Measures who is represented in four common expression datasets and how fairly six models treat age, gender and race groups. The datasets are heavily skewed, and the two most accurate models in the study were also the most biased.

    Reliability: Medium-high. A preprint, so not yet peer-reviewed in the version I read, but from the research group that created AffectNet and with a transparent method.

    Cited in: Successes and failures, The challenges

  19. [19] “Professional actors demonstrate variability, not stereotypical expressions, when portraying emotional states in photographs”, T. Le Mau, K. Hoemann, S. H. Lyons, J. M. B. Fugate, E. N. Brown, M. Gendron, and L. F. Barrett. Nature Communications, vol. 12, article 5037. August 2021.

    https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-25352-6

    Synopsis: Analyses 604 photographs of professional actors portraying emotions in described situations, coded with Action Units. The actors used many different face configurations for the same emotion. Only fear, happiness and surprise were portrayed with moderate consistency, and people judged the emotion better when they knew the situation.

    Reliability: High. Peer-reviewed in a leading science journal, by a well-known emotion research group. It challenges the basic assumption behind emotion labels, so I use it in the limitations chapter.

    Cited in: The challenges

  20. [20] “Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act)”, European Parliament and Council of the European Union. Official Journal of the European Union. 12 July 2024.

    https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj

    Synopsis: The European Union's law on artificial intelligence. Article 5(1)(f) prohibits AI systems that infer people's emotions in workplaces and schools, with an exception for medical or safety reasons. The prohibitions apply from 2 February 2025.

    Reliability: High. It is the primary legal text. I am not a lawyer, and how the rule applies to an assistive device is a question for one.

    Cited in: The challenges
