Successes and failures

How well does this work? The answer depends on where you test. This chapter puts the numbers from the papers side by side and separates what works from what still fails.

2 minute readResultsComparison
Read the narration text

Chapter seven, successes and failures.

I compared six systems from the research. Two are built for covered and turned faces. One is built for video. One is built for phones. And two are built for blind and low-vision users.

The first finding is that no single system does everything. The systems that handle covered faces were not tested on phones. The phone system was not tested on covered faces. And the two assistive systems were not tested by blind or low-vision users at all.

The chart shows accuracy on covered and turned faces for three test sets. Region attention helps in every case, but look at the third panel. On the hardest test set, accuracy on covered faces is below sixty percent.

So where does it work? On clear front views, in good light, and for strong expressions like a big smile. Where does it fail? On natural photos from the web, on rare expressions such as disgust, and whenever the test data differ from the training data. One recent model scored ninety-eight percent on its own test set and about seventy-two percent on a standard one.

What the table shows No single system does everything. The methods for covered faces were not measured on phones, and the phone models were not measured on covered faces. Neither assistive system was tried by the people it is meant for: the most recent paper says that it “does not currently include any data from visually impaired participants” [6].

Attention helps in every case. But on natural photos the level stays low: on AffectNet even the better method is right on fewer than six covered faces in ten.

Where it works

  • Clear front views of strong expressions. The best models pass 92% on RAF-DB [2].
  • Test data that look like the training data. About 98% on a model's own cleaned set [6].
  • Speed. The smallest network needs 16 ms per face on a phone [9], less than the 33 ms between two frames.
  • Smiles. Strong, easy to see when the mouth is visible, and plentiful in the training data [18].

Where it fails

  • Natural photos. Leading methods are near 67% on AffectNet [2]. The model that scored 98% on its own set fell to about 72% there [6].
  • Natural video. The best listed DFEW result is 77.51% WAR and 67.01% UAR [2], on movie clips that are easier than a worn camera's view.
  • Rare expressions. A strong video model recognises disgust 18.62% of the time [12].
  • Covered mouths. A method built for masks reaches 69.3% [5], and people themselves lose 31% [15].
  • The real task. None of the six reports results from live conversations filmed by a worn camera.
In short The parts exist: small fast classifiers, attention for covered faces, smoothing for video. Previous work has not yet put them together on a wearable and tested the result with BLV users.
