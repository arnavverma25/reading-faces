Sensor possibilities

The camera decides what the software can ever see. This chapter compares where the camera can sit, what it delivers, and where the computing should happen.

3 minute readHardware
Read the narration text

Chapter two, sensor possibilities.

There are three realistic places for the camera. In the hand, on the chest, or on a pair of glasses. A phone in the hand has a good camera, but a blind user cannot check where it points. A phone on the chest is hands-free, but it looks up at the face from below. Camera glasses follow the head and give the best view, but they have a small battery and little computing power.

The second question is how big the face is in the picture. I worked this out with a simple formula. With a normal phone lens, a face two metres away is about one hundred pixels wide. With a wide-angle lens it is only about sixty pixels. So a wider lens finds faces more easily but sees less detail.

The third question is where the computing runs. Published prototypes for blind users send the video to a server. I recommend a different split: the camera on the glasses and the computing on the phone in the pocket, so that the video of other people never leaves the user's own device.

The parts and what they deliver

Every option has the same parts: a lens that sets the field of view, an image sensor, a processor, a battery, an output (earbud or vibration motor), and a wireless link if camera and processor are apart.

Output format. The sensor delivers frames, commonly colour images of 1920 by 1080 pixels, 30 times per second. The neural networks use much smaller pictures. In Google's on-device face pipeline, the detector takes a 192 by 192 pixel image and the landmark model a 256 by 256 pixel crop of the face [4].

How big is the face in the picture?

This number matters most, and you can work it out in advance:

face pixels = image width × face width ÷ (2 × distance × tan(field of view ÷ 2))

I assume a face 15 cm wide and a frame 1920 pixels wide, and compare a normal phone lens (about 70°) with a wide-angle lens (about 100°).

A wide lens forgives poor aim, which helps a user who cannot see the screen. The price is detail: at two metres the face is about 60 pixels wide and must be enlarged four times for the landmark model.

My working rule Keep the face at least 100 pixels wide: within about 2 metres for the normal lens and 1.2 metres for the wide one. This is my own estimate, not a published limit.

Frame rate

At 30 frames per second, an ordinary expression of 0.5 to 4 seconds covers 15 to 120 frames. A micro-expression of 0.065 to 0.5 seconds covers only 2 to 15, and the research datasets for those were mostly filmed at 60 to 200 frames per second [3]. A phone camera suits ordinary expressions only.

Where should the computing happen?

PlaceGoodBad
On the glassesNothing else to carryTiny battery, weak processor, heat near the face
On the phoneStrong processor, large battery, video stays with the userNeeds a wireless link
On a serverAny model sizeNetwork delay, and video of other people leaves the device

Both published systems for visually impaired users lean on big hardware. In one, a server does the work and sends back speech [5]. In the other, the model is 331 MB and untested on a small device [6].

What I recommend Camera on the glasses, computing on the phone, nothing sent to a server. This assumes enough light, a partner within about 2 metres, and a sound that tells the user when a face is found or lost.
