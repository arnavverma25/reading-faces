Video and changing emotions

A conversation is a film, not a photograph. Expressions build up, hold and fade, and a label that jumps around thirty times a second is useless to a listener.

3 minute readVideoAlgorithms
Read the narration text

Chapter four, video and changing emotions.

An expression is an event in time. It rises, which is called the onset. It holds at its peak, the apex. And it fades, the offset. An ordinary smile lasts between half a second and four seconds.

If a classifier looks at each frame alone, its label flickers. For a blind listener, every flicker would be a wrong spoken word.

There are three levels of using time. Level one is cheap: keep a running average of the scores and speak only when the average is clearly above a threshold. In one study, averaging over fifteen frames raised the score from zero point three eight to zero point four zero. The price is a short delay. Level two is a model that watches a clip of sixteen frames at once. It is the most accurate, but it is far too heavy for glasses. Level three is to look at fewer frames when the answer is already clear, which saves battery.

For a phone I recommend level one, with the idea from level three.

Micro-expressions are too short for a normal camera. This tutorial is about ordinary expressions.

An expression is an event in time

An expression rises (onset), holds at its strongest point (apex) and fades (offset). An ordinary one lasts roughly 0.5 to 4 seconds [3], or 15 to 120 frames.

The problem: labels that flicker

A classifier that looks at one frame at a time makes independent mistakes in each frame. Conversation makes it worse: during onset and offset the face is between two expressions, talking moves the lips and jaw, and the camera moves.

For a blind listener every change would be a spoken word, and most would be wrong.

Three levels of using time

Level 1: smooth the frame-by-frame scores

Keep a running average s of the newest frame's scores p:

s = a × p + (1 − a) × s    with   a = 2 ÷ (N + 1)   for a window of N frames

Then add two rules. Speak only when the highest average passes a threshold. And let a chosen label stay until it drops a little below that threshold, so it does not switch on and off at the edge (hysteresis).

In a paper aimed at mobile devices, averaging over 15 frames raised the expression F1 score on the Aff-Wild2 validation videos from 0.381 to 0.402 [11]. The cost is delay: 15 frames hold half a second of history.

Level 2: a model that watches a clip

Give the network a short clip so it sees the movement itself. MAE-DFER takes 16 frames. It first learns from about one million unlabelled face videos by rebuilding hidden parts of them, then is fine-tuned on labelled clips [12]. On the DFEW movie-clip benchmark it scores 74.43% WAR and 63.41% UAR [12], against 56.51% and 45.35% for the first baseline in 2020 [13]. The best result in a 2024 survey is 77.51% and 67.01% [2].

The cost is size: about 85 million parameters and 50 billion arithmetic operations per clip [12]. That is server hardware, not glasses.

Level 3: look at fewer frames

Most of the time nothing changes on the face. An adaptive frame rate starts with a few frames and adds more only while the decision is unreliable. It is reported to be 1.5 to 40 times faster than processing every frame [14].

My choice Level 1 on the phone, with the level 3 idea of slowing down when the face is steady: together they cost almost nothing. Level 2 gives the best numbers and suits a strong processor, but I found no report of it running on a wearable.

Reading the scores: WAR and UAR

WAR (weighted average recall) is plain accuracy over all clips. UAR (unweighted average recall) averages the accuracy of each class, so a rare class counts as much as a common one.

UAR is lower because rare expressions are hard. Disgust makes up 1.22% of the single-label DFEW clips [13], and MAE-DFER recognises it 18.62% of the time [12]. For an assistive tool the rare expressions often matter most, so UAR is the more honest number.

What about micro-expressions?

A micro-expression lasts 0.065 to 0.5 seconds [3]: 2 to 15 frames at 30 frames per second, and the movement is weak. The research datasets were filmed in the laboratory at up to 200 frames per second and are small. CASME II has 247 samples [3]. A moving phone camera cannot do this job, so this tutorial is about ordinary expressions.
